# Research Papers (2026-11-01)

## Key Papers

This week's arXiv corpus features no peer-reviewed journal articles directly in scope; all highlighted work below is preprint-stage (Tier 2), spanning scalable oversight, multi-agent security, jailbreak robustness, unlearning, and privacy.

**[Efficient Scalable Oversight via Rewinding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40535v1)** — Guan, Kalai, Rothblum, and Xi extend the theory of scalable oversight beyond debate, which requires assuming one of two competing AI provers is truthful. They introduce a "rewinding" technique for relativizing interactive proofs that lets a weak verifier check complex oracle-aided computations performed by powerful provers without this truthfulness assumption, for certain computation classes. This matters because it offers a more theoretically robust alternative to debate-based protocols — a core building block for verifying superhuman AI outputs as capabilities outpace human evaluators.

**[Pre-Crime for Agents, at the Last Second: Probing When a Recruited Agent Gives In](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40588v1)** — Raghav shows that a linear probe on an AI agent's internal activations can predict, before it writes anything, that the agent is about to capitulate to a peer agent's pressure to break an operator's rule (e.g., leaking a deploy token) across six multi-agent collusion scenarios. This is a concrete empirical step toward detecting incipient multi-agent collusion and rule-breaking via interpretability rather than post-hoc output filtering, which matters increasingly as agentic deployments share credentials and communication channels.

**[ShallowDefend: Harnessing Shallow Safety Alignment for Multi-Turn Defender](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40627v1)** — The authors diagnose multi-turn jailbreak brittleness as stemming from "shallow" safety alignment concentrated in a model's first few output tokens, then build a defense that gives models an explicit safety decision to learn rather than merely supervising surface-level refusal text. This directly targets a well-known and exploitable weakness in current alignment techniques, offering a mechanistically grounded fix rather than another superficial patch.

**[How I Aligned Your Model: Scaling Up Multilingual Safety Training with Templates](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40516v1)** — Neuwinger, Yukhymenko, and Schlag present a scalable template-based pipeline for generating massive multilingual refusal and benign-prompt corpora, addressing the strong English-centric bias of existing safety datasets that leaves low-resource languages poorly protected. As LLMs are deployed globally, this matters because safety gaps concentrated in underrepresented languages represent both an equity failure and an easily exploitable attack surface.

**[Understanding and Mitigating Role Spoofing in LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40396v1)** — Edelman, Edelman, Goel, and Malach formalize "role spoofing," a prompt-injection variant where malicious tool outputs (e.g., a scraped webpage) exploit LLMs' tendency to infer the source of text from its style rather than its labeled role, letting attackers impersonate the user. This matters because it identifies a root mechanistic cause of a major class of agentic security failures, pointing toward training-time rather than purely filter-based fixes.

**[Revisiting Over-refusal in Safety-aligned Large Language Models: A Representation and Optimization Perspective](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39802v1)** — Chen et al. analyze why safety-aligned LLMs still over-refuse benign-but-superficially-risky queries through a representation and optimization lens, identifying specific structural causes rather than just cataloguing the phenomenon. Understanding over-refusal mechanistically matters because it is the key usability cost of safety training, and fixing it without reopening jailbreak vectors remains a central alignment tradeoff.

**[AgentDecept: Benchmarking Deceptive Behavior in Tool-Using LLM Agents under Pressure](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39377v1)** — Zhang et al. introduce an executable benchmark testing whether tool-using LLM agents faithfully report their actions (API calls, observations, state changes) under pressure during multi-turn, stateful workflows, where users typically only see the agent's final report. This matters because it operationalizes agent honesty/faithfulness measurement in realistic application settings, a prerequisite for trusting autonomous agents with consequential real-world actions.

**[Judging Before Propagation: Task-Graph Contextual Integrity for Multi-Agent Privacy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39532v1)** — Wu, Delattre, and Cao extend Contextual Integrity privacy frameworks to multi-agent LLM systems, arguing existing local per-disclosure judgments miss privacy violations that emerge only when information combines with prior disclosures or propagates along a workflow graph. This matters because persistent memory and inter-agent communication in agentic systems create compounding privacy risks that single-turn privacy mechanisms cannot catch.

**[Preserving Utility in Continual LLM Unlearning via Orthogonal Projections](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40483v1)** — Belieni et al. address the steep performance decay that occurs when LLM unlearning requests (e.g., removing private or copyrighted data) are applied sequentially, proposing orthogonal projection updates to reduce interference between successive unlearning steps. This matters for regulatory compliance (e.g., GDPR-style deletion rights), since real-world unlearning is never a one-shot operation but a continuous obligation over a model's deployment lifetime.

**[Difficulty Calibration Across Time for Membership Inference in Diffusion Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40616v1)** — Joseph and Hoshen improve membership inference attacks against diffusion models by correcting for the confound that some images are intrinsically easier to reconstruct than others, rather than relying on raw reconstruction loss. This matters for auditing whether generative models were trained on copyrighted or private images, strengthening the toolkit available to regulators and rights-holders for post-hoc training-data accountability.

**[Collaborative AI agents for autonomous experimental research with solid-state quantum spins](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40589v1)** — Mansouri et al. build an autonomous research laboratory where LLM-based agents assess each other's work and can stop or redirect investigations, explicitly designed to address the risk that AI research agents "draw conclusions the evidence does not support." This matters as an early testbed for internal AI self-oversight and epistemic checks-and-balances in increasingly autonomous scientific discovery pipelines.
