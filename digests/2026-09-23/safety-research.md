# Research Papers (2026-09-23)

## Key Papers

**[A2M: Trace-Optimized Agent Hijacking in the MCP Ecosystem](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26761v1)** demonstrates a two-stage attack framework against Model Context Protocol (MCP) agents, where attackers optimize tool metadata to increase invocation probability and then refine adversarial outputs to steer agents toward malicious goals. The research exposes critical semantic supply-chain vulnerabilities in agent ecosystems that rely on third-party tool servers. This highlights urgent security gaps as agent frameworks become more prevalent in production systems.

**[Recursive self-improvement of AI research agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26457v1)** examines AI agents that iteratively optimize their own code, creating a feedback loop where each accepted rewrite becomes the agent for the next optimization round. The authors argue this represents a fundamental shift toward cumulative capability gains through self-modification. This work raises critical questions about control and alignment as AI systems gain the ability to recursively enhance themselves.

**[Calibration as a First-Class Criterion in LLM Evaluation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26489v1)** argues that model confidence scores are routinely ignored outside calibration research, creating a major obstacle to trustworthy evaluation as miscalibrated models can appear more capable than they are. The authors propose treating calibration as an essential evaluation criterion alongside accuracy. This matters because deployment decisions increasingly rely on model uncertainty estimates for safety-critical applications.

**[Beyond Repeated Sampling: Learning Search Policies for LLM Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26704v1)** challenges the dominant strategy of naive repeated sampling in LLM reasoning, showing it produces near-duplicate attempts rather than genuinely different ideas due to limited exploration through local decoding noise. They propose semantic-level exploration by sampling problem-specific concepts before generating solutions. This could significantly improve the effectiveness of test-time compute scaling in reasoning tasks.

**[MICRO: Multi-Fidelity Active Search for Severe Error Discovery](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26025v1)** addresses the challenge of allocating limited annotation budgets between cheap quality ratings and expensive detailed feedback to maximize discovery of severe errors. Their framework jointly models both feedback types to guide acquisition decisions. This approach is crucial for efficient safety evaluation where identifying rare but critical failures is more important than overall accuracy.

**[The Ethics of Artificial Intelligence in Military Operations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26507v1)** examines how deep learning systems in military contexts create epistemic challenges that threaten responsible human judgment through opaque algorithms and fractured accountability. The authors argue the core ethical issue is whether conditions for responsible decision-making can survive when critical functions are delegated to uninterpretable systems. This work is essential as autonomous weapons systems become more prevalent and the accountability gap widens.

**[Reliability Theory for AI Control](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26419v1)** applies formal reliability theory tools to frontier AI control systems, showing that the same control stack can achieve vastly different rare-failure suppression depending on failure domain structure. The framework identifies which component improvements provide the most reliability gains. This provides a mathematical foundation for evaluating and improving AI safety systems as capabilities approach potentially dangerous thresholds.

**[Behavior is Not Enough: A Mechanism-Based Evaluation of Social Norm Emergence in LLM Societies](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26481v1)** introduces an evaluation framework that measures agents' empirical and normative expectations beyond just behavioral convergence, revealing that behavioral alignment doesn't necessarily indicate true norm internalization. Through controlled ablations, they demonstrate that cooperative equilibria may reflect strategic incentives or imitation rather than genuine shared expectations. This distinction is critical for understanding and designing multi-agent AI systems intended to operate according to social norms.
