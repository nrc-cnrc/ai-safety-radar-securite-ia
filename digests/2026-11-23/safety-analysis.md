# Blogs & News (2026-11-23)

## Top Stories

**[Import AI 482: Huawei kernel benchmarks; airborne drone detection; and a national lab's AI science benchmark](https://importai.substack.com/p/import-ai-482-huawei-kernel-benchmarks)** — Jack Clark's newsletter rounds up a diverse set of technical developments, including new benchmarks for AI-generated GPU kernels on Huawei hardware, drone detection systems, and a national-lab benchmark probing whether AI can contribute to emergent scientific reasoning. This matters because it signals the diffusion of frontier-adjacent AI capability benchmarking into national-security and scientific domains, areas where capability gains have direct dual-use and governance implications.

**[Obstacles to mindreading: Simple, irreducible, and instrumental opacity](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/3znHBDGQWBjhWEKcQ/obstacles-to-mindreading-simple-irreducible-and-instrumental)** — This post examines the conceptual limits of modeling other agents (human or AI) as goal-seekers, distinguishing between opacity that arises from simple complexity, opacity that is fundamentally irreducible, and opacity that serves an instrumental purpose for the agent being modeled. This matters for interpretability research because it clarifies why some forms of "reading" an AI system's internal goals may be structurally harder than others, informing realistic expectations for mechanistic interpretability and deception-detection work.

**[Moderately cursed questions of AI safety](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/FXGukrjX3bWCi7ycR/moderately-cursed-questions-of-ai-safety)** — The post argues that AI safety culture has an implicit taboo suppressing discussion of uncomfortable but important open questions (short of true "extremely cursed" s-risk topics), and calls for more decoupling norms so these questions can be raised explicitly rather than left to private, siloed conclusions. This matters because unexamined groupthink or self-censorship within the safety community could cause blind spots in research prioritization and risk assessment.

**[The Disappointment of a Satisfying Explanation](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/uK7buQSGjSrbyFw5k/the-disappointment-of-a-satisfying-explanation)** — Revisiting Turing's catalogue of "arguments from various disabilities" (claims that machines will never do X — be kind, creative, fall in love, etc.), this piece reflects on how satisfying explanations for AI behavior can feel deflating once found, with implications for how we judge progress toward general intelligence. This matters as a reminder that intuitive human benchmarks for "real" intelligence or agency continue to shift as capabilities advance, complicating public and expert risk communication.

## Policy & Governance

**[Judge the Cyber Apocalypse by the Data, Not the Headlines](https://cset.georgetown.edu/article/judge-the-cyber-apocalypse-by-the-data-not-the-headlines/)** (Georgetown CSET) — CSET's analysis finds that while technical metrics show AI is currently benefiting cyber attackers more than defenders and is increasingly used in real attacks (consistent with "vulnpocalypse" and "Black October" narratives), real-world impact data tells a less alarming story — suggesting attribution and enforcement may matter as much as raw technical capability in determining actual harm. This matters for policymakers because it cautions against overreacting to capability-based threat narratives without corresponding evidence of realized harm, and points toward deterrence and enforcement mechanisms as a underexplored lever in AI cyber-risk governance.
