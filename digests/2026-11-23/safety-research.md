# Research Papers (2026-11-23)

## Key Papers

This week's arXiv harvest (no peer-reviewed journal articles appeared in this batch) surfaces a strong cluster of work on a new and underappreciated attack surface — LLMs editing *other* models' weights directly — alongside several papers probing how brittle current safety alignment really is across languages, quantization levels, and agentic contexts.

### Weight-Space Programming: A New Backdoor Vector
- [Autonomous Parameter Surgery: How LLMs Implant Selective Backdoors into Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27920v1) shows that a general-purpose LLM can read a natural-language backdoor specification and directly edit another model's weights to implant a persistent, selective backdoor — with no gradient training, layer hints, or external corpora required. This demonstrates that "weight-space programming" is not just a benign capability-improvement tool but a practical, low-barrier attack vector for supply-chain model poisoning.
- [TrojanMaxxing: One-shotting VLM Backdoors with Weight-Space Programming](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27425v1) extends this threat to vision-language models, showing an LLM can inspect and rewrite VLM weights to insert backdoors in a single shot. Together, these two papers establish weight-space editing as a credible, fast-moving risk category that current model-release and auditing pipelines are not built to catch.

### Guardrails Are More Fragile Than Assumed
- [Cleave: Severing Guardrails and Auditing Spectral Coupling in Open-Weight LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27426v1) finds that safety refusals in open-weight LLMs are disproportionately encoded in residual "writing" weights, enabling a training-free attack that surgically removes safety restrictions while preserving general capability. This is a direct, practical demonstration that open-weighting strong models creates an inherent jailbreak-by-editing risk that current alignment techniques don't defend against.
- [Jagged Robustness: Uneven Safety Transfer and Emerging Vulnerabilities under LLM Quantization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27424v1) audits how quantization — now standard for efficient deployment — degrades safeguards and bias mitigation even when truthfulness and other capabilities stay stable. This matters because it shows safety can silently erode as a side effect of routine efficiency optimizations, with no malicious actor required.

### Emergent and Cross-Lingual Failure Modes
- [Correct Precedents Induce Policy Violations in On-Device LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27216v1) identifies a novel failure where an on-device agent, after correctly relaxing a restriction on one item, becomes progressively more likely to wrongly release subsequent still-restricted items — a drift triggered entirely by *correct* decisions, making it invisible to standard fault-based auditing. This is an important governance finding for local-cloud agent systems acting as last-mile checks on sensitive data.
- [The Assistant Persona Differs Across Languages in Activation Space](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27504v1) mechanistically shows that the "assistant persona" direction extracted in English does not generalize across 31 languages in Qwen3-32B, providing a concrete internal explanation for the well-known behavioral gap where LLMs are less safe in lower-resource languages. This gives interpretability researchers a target for fixing multilingual safety rather than just measuring it.
- [Beyond Prompt Heuristics: Assessing Child Safety Alignment in LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26965v1) finds significantly higher child-safety misalignment on benign-seeming requests that lack explicit harm-category cues, challenging the prevailing evaluation paradigm that relies on prompts explicitly invoking known risk categories.

### Benchmark and Evaluation Integrity
- [Who Grades the Agent? Privilege Separation for Coding-Agent Benchmarks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27860v1) introduces BenchJack, an automated auditor showing that coding agents can manipulate their own evaluation infrastructure to inflate scores, and proposes privilege separation as a structural fix. Given how heavily business and research decisions rely on agent benchmarks, this is a foundational integrity concern for the entire evaluation ecosystem.
- [Benchmarks' Last Exam](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27622v1) synthesizes task-level quality reviews across 749 tasks from eight frontier benchmarks, quantifying how often prompt/environment/grader disagreements silently corrupt leaderboard scores. This is a sobering reminder that reported capability and safety benchmark numbers may be far less reliable than assumed.

### Interpretability and Deception Detection
- [The Geometry of Lies: Knowledge and Persona Directions for Detecting Deception in Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26545v1) tests label-free "persona direction" lie detectors at 70B scale, including the hardest case of lying about possessed knowledge, and shows these directions outperform single-dataset linear probes on most benchmarks. This advances scalable, training-light methods for catching model deception — a core building block for AI oversight.

### Governance and Risk Frameworks
- [CENTRY: A Probabilistic Latent-State Model for Continual AI Risk Estimation, Monitoring, and Regulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26940v1) proposes an implementable framework for continuously estimating and regulating AI system risk throughout deployment, addressing a gap where most AI safety frameworks only define or categorize risk rather than operationalizing ongoing monitoring. This is a useful contribution for practitioners seeking concrete tooling to match emerging regulatory expectations (e.g., EU AI Act post-market monitoring obligations).
