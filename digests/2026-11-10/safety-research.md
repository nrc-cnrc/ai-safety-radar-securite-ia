# Research Papers (2026-11-10)

## Key Papers

### Governance & Policy

[Frontier AI Safety and Governance 2026: Risk Understanding and Coordinated Governance](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12434v1) is a major multi-stakeholder report from the World Internet Conference's AI Safety and Governance Program, authored by a large international coalition including Seán Ó hÉigeartaigh, Yaodong Yang, and dozens of other researchers and policy figures. It systematically maps the frontier AI risk landscape, assesses severity and response priorities across risk categories, and reviews existing global governance mechanisms to propose coordinated approaches. This matters because it represents one of the most comprehensive attempts to align Chinese and international stakeholders on a shared frontier-risk taxonomy and governance roadmap, a prerequisite for any effective coordinated oversight regime.

[Beyond Falsehoods 2026: Global Trends in the Regulation of Misinformation and Disinformation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12770v1) analyzes 409 coded legislative and policy entries on mis/disinformation from 1995–2025, tracking growth, geographic spread, and regulatory design patterns. The report offers early evidence on which regulatory approaches (hard law vs. soft law vs. policy guidance) are gaining traction globally. It matters as a rare longitudinal, comparative dataset for understanding how governments are actually responding to AI-amplified information harms, useful for benchmarking future AI-specific disinformation rules.

### Auditing, Evaluation & Trust

[Double Blind Evals: Resolving the Dual Confidentiality Dilemma in AI Safety Auditing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12604v1) tackles a core tension in third-party safety auditing: evaluators want to keep test prompts secret to preserve benchmark validity, while model developers want to keep weights and inference code within their own secure infrastructure. The authors (including Andrew Trask, Miles Brundage, and a large cross-institutional team) propose cryptographic protocols enabling "double-blind" evaluations that satisfy both constraints simultaneously. This matters because confidentiality disputes have been a persistent practical barrier to independent frontier model auditing, and a credible technical solution could unlock more rigorous external evaluation regimes.

[How Well Does AI Peer Review Work? A Benchmark Built from 100 Planted Errors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12820v1) plants 100 known errors into 10 psychology papers and tests frontier LLMs and commercial AI review tools, finding the best system catches 71/100 errors alone but ensembling catches 93/100, while omission-type errors remain universally missed. This provides one of the first controlled, ground-truth benchmarks for AI-assisted scientific quality control. It matters because as AI tools are increasingly used (or proposed) for peer review and research integrity checks, understanding their systematic blind spots (especially omissions) is critical for safe deployment in scientific gatekeeping.

[Multi-Agent Screening: What Agreement and Acceptance Cannot Tell You](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12705v1) formally analyzes what can and cannot be inferred about AI screening agents' error rates (recall/precision) from agreement and human-acceptance signals alone, showing that agreement never proves correctness and acceptance only bounds precision, not recall. This is directly relevant to agentic search, e-discovery, and AI-assisted paper review pipelines where screening errors are silently invisible. It matters because it exposes a fundamental measurement gap in deployed multi-agent screening systems that current evaluation practices systematically overlook.

[Can the Confidence and Decisions of System 1 Models Be Trusted? An Explainability and Perturbation Audit of Jev-Style Decision Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12521v1) introduces a deterministic audit benchmark for "System 1" models that skip autoregressive generation in favor of single-pass typed probability outputs, testing their calibration and explainability robustness under label-preserving perturbations. These models are increasingly deployed for low-latency routing and triage, yet their reliability had not been independently characterized. It matters because it extends safety auditing methodology beyond standard LLMs to a growing class of fast, structured-output decision systems used in production.

### Alignment Methods

[TLRS-DPO: Token-Level Risk Scoring for Better Direct Preference Optimization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11232v1) augments standard sequence-level DPO with fine-grained, offline token-level risk scoring to better localize and suppress unsafe sub-sequences within otherwise acceptable responses. This addresses a known weakness of DPO, that it cannot pinpoint which specific tokens make a response unsafe. It matters because improving the granularity of preference-based alignment could reduce over- or under-suppression of risky content without requiring costly online RLHF.

[AERO: Adaptive Ensemble-Disagreement Routing for Oracle Feedback in Sample-Efficient Online RLHF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10750v1) proposes routing preference-label queries toward contexts with high ensemble disagreement, rather than uniform sampling, to reduce the human/oracle feedback bottleneck in online RLHF. This targets a practical scaling constraint in alignment pipelines. It matters because more sample-efficient RLHF could lower the cost barrier to maintaining well-aligned models as they scale, making rigorous alignment more economically feasible.

### Reliability in High-Stakes Domains

[Evaluating Cross-Signal Coherence in Mammography Vision-Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12574v1) evaluates whether VLMs' stated confidence and written justifications are internally consistent with their answers on breast density/BI-RADS classification, using controlled perturbations across four models and two datasets. The study finds confidence and explanation often diverge from the model's actual answer under perturbation. It matters because clinical deployment of multimodal AI increasingly relies on these auxiliary signals for trust calibration, and incoherence between them could mislead clinicians in high-stakes diagnostic settings.

[Fairness in Explainable AI Narratives: Decomposing Social Disparities in LLM-Generated Explanations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12592v1) examines whether LLM-generated natural-language narratives translating SHAP-style explanations reproduce social bias, even when only proxy variables are present, in high-risk decision domains. Since these narratives are often the primary channel through which affected individuals understand and contest adverse automated decisions, biased explanations compound the harm of biased underlying models. It matters because it surfaces a previously unstudied fairness risk layer sitting on top of already-audited XAI pipelines.
