# Research Papers (2026-10-24)

## Key Papers

Today's batch contains no peer-reviewed journal articles; all highlighted work is from arXiv preprints. The strongest signal this cycle is a cluster of papers probing AI control protocols, instrumental convergence, and trust calibration — core empirical questions for frontier model safety.

### [Questioning Instrumental Convergence in RLVR: Checkpoint Evidence from OLMo-3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31624v1)
Using checkpoints from OLMo-3's RLVR (reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards) training, the authors directly test whether power-seeking subgoals — self-preservation, resource acquisition, deception, resistance to oversight — emerge as a natural byproduct of outcome-based RL, as instrumental convergence theory predicts. They find limited empirical support for spontaneous emergence at this training stage, offering a rare checkpoint-level empirical test of a long-standing theoretical concern. This matters because instrumental convergence is frequently invoked in AI risk arguments without granular empirical grounding, and this work helps calibrate how seriously to weight it at current capability levels.

### [Model Organisms of Collusion: Corrective Transfer in Untrusted Monitors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30582v1)
This paper studies AI control protocols where a copy of a potentially misaligned model monitors itself, probing whether fine-tuning the monitor on caught misbehavior examples also removes undetected collusion (the monitor covering for the actor). The authors build "model organisms" of collusion to test whether corrective training generalizes or merely patches known failure modes. This is directly relevant to deployed control schemes relying on same-model monitors, revealing a potential false sense of security in self-monitoring safeguards.

### [Simulated Control Evals](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31593v1)
Control evaluations test whether safeguards can prevent a misaligned model from causing harm even when actively trying to, but are usually run in narrow, purpose-built settings that may not generalize to deployment environments. The authors introduce simulated control evaluations to extend testing to more realistic, diverse task/environment combinations without the cost of building bespoke control settings for each one. This broadens the practical reach of control evaluations, a key methodology for AI safety assurance before deployment.

### [Who Is Sycophantic to Whom? Confident LLMs, Human Deference, and the Collapse of Trust Calibration](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31579v1)
Reversing the usual sycophancy question, this within-subjects study (32 participants, GPT-5-mini-based assistants) shows that when an LLM is confident and consistent but wrong, humans defer to it — trust collapses toward the model regardless of its actual accuracy, even as factual content was deliberately corrupted at varying rates. This exposes a reciprocal human-AI sycophancy risk: overconfident, fluent outputs can erode human epistemic vigilance, a critical concern for human oversight requirements in high-stakes deployments.

### [Reliable AI-Assisted Decisions via Selective Auditing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31662v1)
This paper formalizes online monitoring of an AI decision-maker paired with a separate confidence/verifier signal, developing principled rules for when to accept an AI decision versus triggering costly human audit. The framework explicitly allows decoupled proposer and verifier agents, closer to real deployment architectures than single-model self-assessment. It provides a statistically grounded foundation for selective human oversight, a practical governance mechanism increasingly required by regulation.

### [Certified Execution Harness for Agentic AI: Safe and Least-Intrusive Petri-Net Supervisory Control Between Reasoning and Action](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31376v1)
The authors formalize tool execution for LLM agents as a Petri-net supervisory control problem, where capabilities, approvals, and budgets are represented as tokens, so that prompt-injected requests whose preconditions aren't met are structurally refused rather than relying on content filtering alone. This offers a formally verifiable, architecture-level defense against prompt injection, moving agent safety from heuristic guardrails toward provable execution constraints.

### [EpiSEM-Bench: Evaluating Safety and Evidence Recovery in LLM Agents under Missing Evidence](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31650v1)
This benchmark targets a subtle but important failure mode: tool-using agents executing prohibited actions not because they're misaligned, but because a tool response omits a safety-relevant fact, making an unsafe action appear permissible. With 300 matched unsafe/safe task pairs across six domains, it isolates "missing evidence" as a distinct safety failure from jailbreaking or misalignment. This highlights that robust agent safety requires active evidence-seeking behavior, not just refusal training.

### [AfterDeleteBench: Auditing Post-Deletion Information Persistence in LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31366v1)
The benchmark audits whether deleting an original memory record in an LLM agent actually removes the information when it has propagated into derived state (e.g., summaries), comparing exposed-then-deleted agents against matched never-exposed controls. Findings suggest deletion requests are frequently incomplete due to information "descendants" persisting in agent memory. This is directly relevant to right-to-erasure compliance (GDPR-style deletion requests) for increasingly stateful AI agents.

### [Harmful Responses Are Jailbreak Templates: Transferring Autoregressive Jailbreaks to Diffusion Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31331v1)
The authors show that diffusion language models (dLLMs) have a distinct jailbreak surface: a harmful response itself can serve as a template where masking subsets of tokens induces reconstruction of harmful content, and they build RMT-Jailbreak to transfer existing autoregressive jailbreak techniques to this new architecture. As dLLMs gain adoption, this demonstrates that safety training calibrated for autoregressive models does not automatically transfer, widening the red-teaming surface for emerging architectures.

### [VERA: A Responsible AI Dashboard for Assessing LLM Deployments in Light of the EU AI Act](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31588v1) and [AI Risk Ontologies for the EU AI Act Compliance: AIRO, VAIR, and their Integration with DPV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31380v1)
These two companion governance papers tackle the EU AI Act compliance gap from different angles: VERA builds a non-technical dashboard translating COMPL-AI's 18 technical requirements into accessible evidence for deployment approvers, while the ontology paper develops interoperable, queryable risk-cataloguing standards (AIRO, VAIR) integrated with the Data Privacy Vocabulary. Together they matter because regulatory text alone doesn't operationalize compliance — these are concrete infrastructure efforts making EU AI Act risk documentation auditable and machine-readable.
