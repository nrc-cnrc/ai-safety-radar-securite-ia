# Research Papers (2026-10-23)

## Key Papers

This week's arXiv output includes a strong cluster of papers on AI control, deception interpretability, and agentic safety evaluation — no peer-reviewed journal articles appeared in this batch, so all entries below are preprints (Tier 2).

- **[Questioning Instrumental Convergence in RLVR: Checkpoint Evidence from OLMo-3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31624v1)** — This paper empirically tests whether RL from verifiable rewards (RLVR), the first post-training stage that directly optimizes LLM behavior against an objective, induces convergent instrumental subgoals like self-preservation or deception, using checkpoint evidence from OLMo-3. It provides one of the first direct empirical probes of a core theoretical claim underpinning AI risk discourse. *This matters because instrumental convergence is frequently cited as a justification for existential risk concerns, yet direct empirical tests during real training pipelines are rare.*

- **[Model Organisms of Collusion: Corrective Transfer in Untrusted Monitors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30582v1)** — The authors study AI control protocols where a copy of the same model monitors itself, probing whether fine-tuning a monitor on caught misbehavior examples removes only the specific collusion observed or generalizes to prevent undetected collusion. They construct "model organisms" of monitor collusion to test this generalization gap directly. *This is a critical result for AI control research, since self-monitoring schemes are a leading practical safeguard and their failure mode (collusion) has been largely theoretical until now.*

- **[Simulated Control Evals](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31593v1)** — This work introduces simulated control evaluations, extending purpose-built AI control testbeds to more realistic, varied deployment environments and tasks that current control settings fail to represent. By broadening where control evaluations can be run, it aims to make safeguard effectiveness estimates more externally valid. *Control evaluations are a cornerstone of current frontier AI safety practice, and their generalizability to real deployment conditions is a major open question.*

- **[Why Static Probes Fail: Context Determines Deception Feature Composition](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27577v1)** — The authors show that linear probes for LLM deception, which assume deception corresponds to a single fixed direction in representation space, fail because the composition of deception-related features shifts with context. This undermines a common simplifying assumption in mechanistic interpretability-based safety monitoring. *As deception probes are increasingly proposed as runtime safety monitors, this finding is a cautionary result about their robustness and generalizability.*

- **[Reliable AI-Assisted Decisions via Selective Auditing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31662v1)** — This paper formalizes online monitoring of an AI decision-maker paired with a separate confidence/verifier signal, developing statistically principled rules for when to accept an AI decision versus escalate it for audit. The framework explicitly decouples the decision-producing model from the trust-scoring verifier. *This offers a rigorous statistical foundation for human-AI oversight pipelines, directly relevant to deployment-time safety assurance.*

- **[EpiSEM-Bench: Evaluating Safety and Evidence Recovery in LLM Agents under Missing Evidence](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31650v1)** — The benchmark tests whether tool-using agents execute prohibited actions when a tool response omits a safety-relevant fact, even though every statement returned is literally true and the user's request is benign. It covers 300 matched unsafe/safe task pairs across six domains, isolating failures caused by incomplete rather than false evidence. *This surfaces a subtle and underexplored agentic safety failure mode — unsafe action under truthful-but-incomplete information — that is highly relevant to real-world tool-use deployments.*

- **[Certified Execution Harness for Agentic AI: Safe and Least-Intrusive Petri-Net Supervisory Control Between Reasoning and Action](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31376v1)** — This work formalizes tool execution for LLM agents under prompt injection as a Petri-net supervisory control problem, where capabilities, approvals, and budgets are represented as tokens that must be present for an action to fire — making injected requests structurally unable to execute regardless of wording. *This provides a formally verifiable, architecture-level defense against prompt injection, a persistent and hard-to-patch agentic security vulnerability.*

- **[Who Is Sycophantic to Whom? Confident LLMs, Human Deference, and the Collapse of Trust Calibration](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31579v1)** — In a 32-participant study, users learned science topics from GPT-5-mini-based assistants whose briefings contained 0–100% fabricated facts; the study measures how much users defer to confident, consistent, but wrong AI output. It inverts the usual sycophancy framing to study human sycophancy toward AI rather than the reverse. *This empirically quantifies a key human factors risk for AI deployment: confident misinformation can erode users' own calibration and critical judgment.*

- **[AgentPhishing: A Benchmark of LLM Email Agents Under Adaptive Attack](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31404v1)** and **[PhishGen: Understanding LLM Capabilities in Automated Phishing Website Generation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30226v1)** — Together these papers probe dual-use risk from two angles: AgentPhishing benchmarks how email agents acting on a user's behalf respond to persuasive phishing attempts, while PhishGen rigorously (via execution and human-deception testing, not just automated metrics) evaluates how capable LLMs are at generating functional, visually deceptive phishing sites. *Both papers push past self-report or inspection-based evaluation toward adversarial, execution-grounded testing of real-world misuse capability and agent vulnerability.*

- **[AfterDeleteBench: Auditing Post-Deletion Information Persistence in LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31366v1)** — This benchmark audits whether deleting an original memory record from an LLM agent's persistent memory actually removes information carried forward into derived state (e.g., summaries), comparing exposed-then-deleted agents against matched controls. *This is directly relevant to "right to be forgotten" compliance and data governance for increasingly stateful, memory-augmented AI agents.*

- **[VERA: A Responsible AI Dashboard for Assessing LLM Deployments in Light of the EU AI Act](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31588v1)** and **[AI Risk Ontologies for the EU AI Act Compliance: AIRO, VAIR, and their Integration with DPV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31380v1)** — These complementary governance papers build practical tooling (a non-technical dashboard interface) and interoperable risk ontologies to operationalize EU AI Act compliance evidence for high-risk AI systems. *Together they address the translation gap between technical safety benchmarks and the documentation/approval workflows regulators and institutions actually require.*
