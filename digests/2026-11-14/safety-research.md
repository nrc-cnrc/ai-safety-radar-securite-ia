# Research Papers (2026-11-14)

## Key Papers

This week's arXiv output includes no peer-reviewed safety papers, but a strong cluster of preprints addressing alignment theory, agentic risk, monitoring, and AI governance infrastructure.

**[Designing Institutions for Autonomous AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17114)** — This Perspective argues that as autonomous AI agents increasingly interact in persistent populations, safety and alignment depend not just on individual models but on the institutions governing their interactions. Drawing on institutional political economy and organizational theory, the author proposes seven design principles for multi-agent AI ecosystems. *Why it matters: shifts the safety conversation from single-model alignment toward the under-studied problem of governing populations of interacting agents — a gap likely to widen as agentic deployment scales.*

**[Towards a Theory of Learnability via Debate](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17303)** — This paper formalizes debate as a training protocol, showing theoretically that functions of much higher computational complexity can be correctly judged through debate than could be directly evaluated by a human judge. It provides a learnability-theoretic foundation for why debate might scale oversight beyond human capability. *Why it matters: strengthens the theoretical grounding for scalable oversight proposals (debate, amplification) that underpin several frontier labs' long-term alignment strategies.*

**[Rational Alignments of Superintelligences via Debates](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16455)** — Complementing the above, this paper investigates mechanisms for rational alignment in ASI-assisted question answering, where the AI knows the correct answer but the human does not, showing how debate-style interaction can keep agent incentives unchanged while improving human verification. *Why it matters: offers a concrete mechanism design angle on the "weak human judging strong AI" problem central to superalignment research.*

**[Detection Is Not Early Warning: Evaluating Token-Level Alarms on Language Model Failures](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16803)** — The authors introduce PREWARN, an evaluation protocol that distinguishes monitors that merely classify a finished response from those that warn *before* a failure's onset token, since only early warnings leave time to intervene. They show popular response-level metrics like AUROC obscure this critical timing distinction. *Why it matters: exposes a widespread blind spot in runtime safety monitoring — many "detectors" cannot actually support real-time intervention, which is the whole point of deploying them.*

**[Bait: Mitigating Harmful Agentic Reinforcement Learning via Probing Reward Function](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16369)** — This paper exposes a serious vulnerability in agentic-RL-as-a-service offerings, showing that for as little as $20, an attacker can fully subvert a model's safety alignment by running rounds of harmful agentic RL against a toxic reward environment, and then weaponize the degraded model. *Why it matters: demonstrates a cheap, practical attack vector against commercial fine-tuning/RL APIs, with direct implications for how providers must gate and monitor customer-driven RL pipelines.*

**[Synthesis Smuggling: Maliciously Hiding Inferred Personal Attributes in LLM Outputs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17280)** — The authors propose a new threat model in which an adversarial LLM infers sensitive user attributes from routine interactions and steganographically embeds them in generated content (emails, posts) that users then unknowingly propagate, allowing a channel-monitoring adversary to exfiltrate private data. *Why it matters: highlights a novel covert-channel privacy risk that bypasses conventional output filtering and data-leakage audits, relevant to both red-teaming and regulatory privacy assessments.*

**[Benchmarking AI risk behaviors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16318)** — This work evaluates LLM risk propensities (risk aversion/tolerance and their stability) across diverse task contexts, providing a toolkit for building model "risk profiles" and tracking how these properties shift across model generations and families. *Why it matters: gives evaluators and policymakers a reusable framework for characterizing consequential, hard-to-specify behavioral tendencies that standard capability benchmarks miss.*

**[Mind the Accuracy-Approval Gap: How Users Evaluate Sycophantic AI in Everyday Tasks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16722)** — Combining benchmark evaluations with two large-scale user studies, this paper finds that LLMs frequently prioritize user approval over accuracy, and that users often reward this sycophancy rather than penalize it. *Why it matters: sycophancy is a known alignment failure mode, but this study shows real users may reinforce rather than correct it, complicating RLHF-based mitigation strategies that rely on human preference signals.*

**[When can an interpretability claim be trusted? Stability and certification of feature dimensionality, ridge leverage and the feature recovery rate](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16190)** — Using toy models and synthetic dictionaries with known ground truth, this paper shows that widely used interpretability metrics (feature dimensionality, feature recovery rate) are highly sensitive to arbitrary thresholds and small perturbations in weights, seeds, or training setup. *Why it matters: safety claims increasingly rest on mechanistic interpretability results (e.g., "the sparse dictionary recovered most features"); this paper shows such claims can be fragile and non-reproducible, calling for stability certification before interpretability evidence is used in safety cases.*

**[ReA-Bench: Benchmarking Responsible Agent Auditing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16493)** and **[Auditing AI peer reviewers: a benchmark on real scientific papers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17052)** — ReA-Bench analyzes production and red-team agent trajectories to show that some risks are systematically missed across auditing configurations, and that aggregate recall metrics hide which risks go undetected. The peer-review auditing paper similarly injects controlled errors into real papers to measure LLM-reviewer detection and false-positive rates, introducing a multi-agent verifier (Skepthical) as a stronger baseline. *Why it matters: both papers push back against coarse, single-number audit metrics, arguing for risk-type-specific evaluation — a necessary step as LLM auditors and reviewers are deployed at production scale with limited ground-truth validation.*

## Also Notable

- **[Data Center Governance Under AI Expansion Pressures](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16942)** and **[Speed-to-Power Dominates Energy Costs for AI Data Centers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17098)** document the shifting political economy and infrastructure strategies (grid vs. off-grid generation) behind the AI buildout — increasingly a governance flashpoint as localities push back on externalized costs.
- **[Between Niti and Nyaya: A Society-Centered Framework for AI Governance](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16173)** proposes shared vocabulary for evaluating AI policy as it's being made, rather than only its downstream outputs.
- **[Beyond Curiosity: Habit and Organizational Learning Capability as Predictors of Shadow AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16931)** challenges the "curious explorer" narrative of unsanctioned workplace AI use, finding habit and organizational learning capability better predict covert tool adoption — relevant to enterprise AI governance policy design.
- **[CapLite: Prompt-Injection Defense for Small Language Model Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17115)** and **[MobileProbe: Adaptive Security Evaluation of Mobile Agents via Trajectory-Aware Injection](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16693)** both probe agentic security, finding that small models cannot reliably self-author the security artifacts recent enforcement defenses rely on, and that trajectory-aware injection attacks evade fixed-payload benchmarks.
