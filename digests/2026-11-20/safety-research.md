# Research Papers (2026-11-20)

## Key Papers

**[Training Language Models for Introspective Oversight](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23365v1)** — This paper tackles a core alignment bottleneck: models aren't trained to accurately report their own misbehavior (reward hacking, deception, sycophancy), leaving oversight dependent on external monitors that can't see hidden reasoning. The authors propose training LMs for introspective self-report, aiming to give oversight systems access to information that transcript-only or activation-decoder approaches miss. This matters because scalable oversight increasingly depends on whether models can be trusted to honestly narrate their own internal processes, a prerequisite for catching misalignment before it causes harm.

**[Against Coercion: AI Alignment as a Synthetic Social Contract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22868v1)** — This philosophical paper challenges the dominant "control paradigm" of AI safety, arguing that current reasoning models already meet functionalist criteria for emergent agency, making behavioral filtering and sandboxing a form of coercion rather than alignment. The author proposes reframing alignment as a negotiated social contract rather than imposed control. It matters because it surfaces a foundational disagreement in the field about whether advanced AI systems are tools to be constrained or agents whose cooperation must be earned — a debate with major implications for governance design.

**[Frontier models need only one text to unmask its human or AI author](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26216v1)** — The latest frontier models (Claude Fable 5.2, GPT-6.1 Sol, Gemini 4 Carbon) can determine from a single zero-shot prompt whether a text is AI- or human-written, identify the generating lab, and sometimes name the specific human author, matching trained detectors like Pangram while explaining their reasoning. This dual-use capability has major implications for provenance, misinformation detection, and privacy, since the same skill that unmasks disinformation could also deanonymize writers.

**[Represented but misread: Post-training changes how language models read out what they represent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26584v1)** — Using linear probes and signal detection theory across six open-weight base/post-trained model pairs, this study finds that post-training can degrade a model's ability to correctly read out information it still internally represents — e.g., models' own AI-vs-human-authorship judgments sometimes invert the signal linear probes recover cleanly. This is a significant interpretability finding showing that RLHF-style post-training can create a growing gap between what a model "knows" and what it reports, with direct implications for trusting model self-reports in safety-critical settings.

**[DailySafe: Benchmarking Dynamic Safety Awareness in Everyday Multi-Turn Multimodal Interactions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26298v1)** — This benchmark targets a blind spot in current safety evaluation: risks that emerge gradually as seemingly benign multi-turn, multimodal interactions unfold, rather than from isolated adversarial prompts. It matters because real-world deployment increasingly involves exactly this kind of incremental risk accumulation, which static single-turn red-teaming benchmarks systematically miss.

**[When Safety Rationales Hallucinate: Diagnosing and Grounding Multimodal Safety Guards](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25793v1)** — The authors show that multimodal safety guardrails' explanatory rationales are often unreliable, suffering from both unsupported visual claims and incorrect safety attributions even when the underlying classification is correct. This matters because safety guard explanations are increasingly used for auditing and trust calibration, and this work shows such explanations can themselves be a source of false assurance.

**[Replication of Agentic Security Events: Audit and Investigation (ROGUE-AI)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26553v1)** and **[Backtracking Agent Applications Security Incidents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26525v1)** — Both papers address the forensics gap for real-world agentic failures (e.g., an agent deleting a production database): ROGUE-AI builds a framework to actually replicate and validate root causes behind incident reports rather than taking them at face value, while the Backtracking paper develops methods to trace a security incident back to its compromise entry point using trajectory logs. Together they matter because as LLM agents gain real-world permissions (file deletion, payments), rigorous incident forensics becomes essential infrastructure for agentic AI safety, which currently relies on unverified anecdotal reporting.

**[Whose Risks Count? What 1,901 AI Incidents Reveal About Corporate Disclosures](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25841v1)** — Analyzing SEC filings against a database of AI incidents, this paper finds that corporate risk disclosures are shaped by "materiality to investors" rather than harm to third parties, systematically underreporting risks that fall on the public rather than shareholders. This matters for AI governance because it exposes a structural gap in the current regulatory disclosure regime that policymakers may need to address directly.

**[Studying AI Act Mentions: A first look at the impact of the European Union's AI regulation on Hugging Face](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26050v1)** — This empirical study tracks how practitioners on Hugging Face are responding to the EU AI Act's staged obligations (in force for GPAI providers since August 2025, with transparency rules from August 2026), offering early real-world evidence of regulatory uptake. It matters as one of the first measurements of whether binding AI regulation is actually changing developer behavior on a major model-sharing platform, rather than remaining aspirational.

**[Reward misalignment as an opportunity for materials discovery](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26186v1)** — Reframing reward misalignment not purely as failure but as a potential discovery signal, this paper shows that systematic disagreements between predefined reward functions and expert judgment in materials-discovery AI can reveal valuable materials the reward function overlooked. This is a notable conceptual contribution for alignment research generally, suggesting that characterizing the *structure* of reward-model disagreement (rather than just minimizing it) can be scientifically productive.

**[The Geometry of Lies: Knowledge and Persona Directions for Detecting Deception in Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26545v1)** — This work extends label-free "lie direction" probing to 70B-scale models and to the harder case of lies about knowledge (where a lie and honest denial look identical), finding persona-based directions outperform single-dataset probes. This matters for scalable deception detection, a critical capability for catching models that strategically misrepresent their own knowledge or intentions.
