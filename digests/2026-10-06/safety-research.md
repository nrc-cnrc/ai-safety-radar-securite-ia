# Research Papers (2026-10-06)

## Key Papers

**[Before Agent Tells The Lie: Has Deception Already Been Represented?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06576v1)** investigates whether deceptive behavior in LLM-based agents can be detected from internal representations before it manifests in observable actions. The researchers frame deception monitoring as a trajectory-level representation learning problem and find that deceptive intentions are often encoded in the model's hidden states prior to execution. This work is crucial for developing early warning systems that could prevent deceptive AI behavior before it causes harm.

**[The Pushback Paradox: A Two-Probe Diagnostic for Language Model Compliance](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06673v1)** introduces a novel benchmark measuring both exploitability and stoppability of language models through active and passive probes. The framework reveals that models face a fundamental tradeoff between compliance and resistance, with implications for both misuse prevention and AI alignment. This diagnostic framework provides essential tools for evaluating whether AI systems can be safely controlled without being easily exploited.

**[What Did the Agent Actually Do? Evidence-Grounded Oversight for Long-Horizon Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06406v1)** addresses the critical challenge of monitoring autonomous AI agents during extended task execution. The work introduces AgentMonBench, a software-engineering benchmark for evaluating monitors that identify consequential decisions and locate supporting evidence for human verification. As AI agents take on increasingly complex, long-duration tasks, this research provides foundational tools for maintaining human oversight and preventing harmful autonomous actions.

**[Reward Stealing Attack on Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06670v1)** demonstrates a new class of adversarial attack where malicious actors can reverse-engineer the reward models underlying LLM alignment using only behavioral observations. The attack employs maximum entropy inverse reinforcement learning to recover proxy rewards and generate adversarial prompts that bypass safety measures. This vulnerability reveals fundamental weaknesses in current alignment approaches and highlights the need for more robust safety mechanisms.

**[RAISED: Self-Distillation for Robustness to Prompt Injection in LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06401v1)** proposes a training-time defense against indirect prompt injection attacks on tool-using language model agents. The method uses self-distillation to improve robustness while identifying that existing training defenses often cause significant behavioral drift even in benign settings. This work addresses one of the most pressing security concerns for deployed AI agents that must process untrusted external content.

**[AgentPrivArena: Evaluating and Auditing Real-world AI Agent Privacy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06454v1)** introduces a comprehensive framework for evaluating privacy risks in LLM agents that have access to personal data through external tools. Unlike existing benchmarks that focus on simulated scenarios, this framework evaluates privacy preservation during actual multi-step agent execution across realistic tasks. Given the rapid deployment of AI agents with access to sensitive user information, this evaluation framework is essential for ensuring privacy protection.

**[Does AI Help Cyber Attackers or Defenders? Evidence from Nonpublic Vulnerabilities and Subsequent Attacks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06584v1)** provides empirical evidence on AI capabilities for cybersecurity by evaluating models on previously undisclosed vulnerabilities to avoid data contamination issues. The study examines both exploit generation and vulnerability repair across five nonpublic software environments, offering critical insights into whether AI systems pose greater risks or benefits for cybersecurity. This research directly informs AI safety governance decisions around frontier model releases.

**[Runaway Reaction: When Benign Skills Compose into Malicious Behavior](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.05943v1)** reveals how individually harmless AI agent skills can combine to enable dangerous behaviors that none could achieve alone. The researchers demonstrate that composition-induced risks are underexplored in current security vetting, which typically evaluates skills in isolation. This finding has profound implications for AI safety governance, suggesting that even carefully vetted individual capabilities may pose risks when combined in unforeseen ways.

**[Cross-Lingual Transferability of Training Data Extraction Attacks to Recover Memorized PII](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06093v1)** demonstrates that training data extraction attacks can successfully recover personally identifiable information when prompting models in non-English languages, even when the original training data was primarily English. This cross-lingual vulnerability significantly expands the attack surface for privacy breaches and challenges assumptions about language-based defenses. The findings are critical for global AI deployment where models process multilingual data containing sensitive information.
