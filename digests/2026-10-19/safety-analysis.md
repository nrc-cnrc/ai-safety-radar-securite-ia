# Blogs & News (2026-10-19)

## Top Stories

- **[The Alignment Problem: Computer Science Meets Analytic Philosophy](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/Emp7awtxsYn8MusNq/the-alignment-problem-computer-science-meets-analytic)** (Alignment Forum): A sweeping conceptual essay attempting to articulate the technical alignment problem by blending theoretical CS/ML theory with analytic philosophy and MIRI/LessWrong-originated framing. The author starts from the observation that "safety" is underspecified until we decide what we want the AI to actually do, introducing a "minimal viable safety" framing to ground the discussion. This matters because foundational conceptual clarity—what exactly are we solving for?—remains a bottleneck for both technical alignment research and the policy frameworks built on top of it.

- **[Deconfusing Corrigibility](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/f8R3B2zqqTJZjSAsm/deconfusing-corrigibility)** (Alignment Forum): A technical deep-dive arguing that despite corrigibility's central role in alignment theory, no consensus definition exists, and most proposed formalizations are flawed. The piece works toward a more rigorous characterization of what it would mean for an AI system to remain correctable/shutdownable by its operators. This matters because corrigibility underpins many proposed safety guarantees (e.g., safe interruptibility, oversight mechanisms), and ambiguity here propagates into weaker real-world safety assurances.

- **[Quick Thoughts on Overhang Arguments](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/aJSdGMzgFRFBcBhGp/quick-thoughts-on-overhang-arguments)** (LessWrong): A critical examination of "overhang" reasoning—the argument pattern used to justify accelerating or publishing capabilities work on the grounds that delaying creates a larger, more dangerous future capability jump. The author questions whether such arguments have ever been validated in other fields and lays out reasons for skepticism. This matters because overhang arguments are frequently invoked inside labs and the safety community to justify risky strategic choices (e.g., open-sourcing, rapid scaling), and this post pushes back on an underexamined piece of community reasoning.

- **[Capital Ownership Will Make You Go Crazy First](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/Stzb33XDsr4uHNerX/capital-ownership-will-make-you-go-crazy-first)** (LessWrong): A speculative piece on AI-driven economic disempowerment, arguing that capital owners—not the jobless masses often assumed to be first disempowered—may actually lose effective agency earlier, through psychological and incentive distortions that come with extreme wealth in an AI-transformed economy. This matters because it complicates simplistic narratives about who bears "disempowerment risk" in automated economies, a question increasingly relevant to governance debates over AI-driven wealth concentration.
