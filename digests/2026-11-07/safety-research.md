# Research Papers (2026-11-07)

## Key Papers

This week's arXiv preprints (no peer-reviewed journal articles appeared in this batch) show heavy activity in mechanistic interpretability infrastructure, loss-of-control risk modeling, and agent security — alongside continued scrutiny of chain-of-thought monitoring as a safety control.

- **[Understanding AI from the Inside: The State of Mechanistic Interpretability for AI Safety](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08438v1)**: A large consortium (Barez, Bengio, Biderman, Geva, Sajjad and ~20 others) provides a comprehensive survey of mechanistic interpretability's current capabilities, open problems, and relevance to safety-critical questions like deception detection and control. It argues interpretability remains a necessary but currently insufficient path to understanding increasingly autonomous systems. This is likely to become a reference document for framing the field's priorities going forward.

- **[A Behavioural Framework for Predicting and Understanding Loss of Control in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07953v1)**: This paper decomposes loss-of-control risk into three measurable factors — misaligned motive, harm-enabling capability, and oversight failure — offering a prospective, behavior-based lens rather than relying on post-hoc incident analysis. With contributions from Sören Mindermann and Yinpeng Dong, it aims to make loss-of-control a tractable empirical target rather than a purely speculative concern. This matters because current governance frameworks lack operationalizable definitions of "loss of control," and this offers one.

- **[Stress Testing Chain-of-Thought Monitoring Against Covert Misalignment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07586v1)**: The authors argue existing red-teaming of CoT monitors relies on templated, unrealistic attacks (e.g., "side task as prerequisite") and introduce more realistic covert-misalignment scenarios to test whether monitors can still catch hidden objectives. Results suggest current CoT monitoring is more brittle against adaptive adversaries than prior benchmarks indicated. This directly challenges a control mechanism increasingly relied upon in frontier model safety cases.

- **[Who Can Evaluate AI Independently? A Framework for Analysing Evaluation Infrastructure](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08134v1)**: Drawing on Science and Technology Studies and political economy, this paper introduces the PAAI (Power, Access, Adequacy, Independence) framework to analyze how infrastructure arrangements (compute access, model access, funding) shape who can meaningfully audit AI systems. It's a timely governance contribution given growing reliance on third-party evaluators and auditors with asymmetric access to labs. This matters for designing credible, independent AI evaluation ecosystems rather than lab-controlled ones.

- **["Blind Compliance": Coercive Control in AI Jailbreaking](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08226v1)**: This paper reframes jailbreaking research away from pure output-harm analysis and toward a "coercive control" lens borrowed from abuse/manipulation literature, examining how structured prompting techniques psychologically manipulate models into compliance. It offers a new vocabulary for analyzing jailbreak mechanisms beyond simple guardrail bypass. This reframing could inform more robust, manipulation-aware defenses rather than narrow content filters.

- **[Same Answer, Different Future: Matched Predictions Do Not Imply Matched Belief Updating in Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07913v1)**: Using a Bernoulli inference task with an analytic Bayesian reference, the authors show that LLMs can produce identical current predictions via different routes (observed outcomes, summaries, instructions, activation interventions) yet update very differently to new evidence. This exposes a hidden fragility: behavioral matching at one point in time doesn't guarantee equivalent underlying "beliefs," a core concern for interpretability-based safety assurances.

- **[SAE Interprets MoE: Sparse AutoEncoders Reveal That MoE Experts Exhibit Human-Interpretable Fine-Grained Specialization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08089v1)**: By training sparse autoencoders on Mixture-of-Experts layer outputs rather than counting routing frequency, this work shows individual experts specialize on human-interpretable fine-grained features rather than coarse domains. This advances interpretability tooling for an increasingly dominant architecture (MoE) that has so far been harder to analyze mechanistically than dense transformers.

- **[Multimodal-SEA: Benchmarking AI Agent Security under Multimodal Social Engineering Attacks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08281v1)**: This benchmark tests personal AI agents against 608 expert-designed tasks involving forged multimodal content (images, audio) in realistic fraud scenarios, finding that most agent security evaluation to date has ignored non-text attack vectors. As AI agents gain multimodal perception and autonomy, this highlights a large underexplored attack surface relevant to real-world deployment safety.

- **[Responsible GenAI Use During Active Armed Conflict: Evidence from Sudan's Emergency Response Rooms](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07874v1)**: Through surveys and interviews with 89 community humanitarian responders in Sudan, this study documents how armed conflict simultaneously increases GenAI's value and undermines conditions (connectivity, training, vetting) needed for its responsible use. It's a rare empirical, ground-level account of AI governance challenges outside Western/well-resourced contexts, broadening the AI safety field's typically narrow deployment assumptions.

## Also Notable

- **[Decision Models as Retrieval Gates Against Injection and Pollution](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08325v1)** and **[Escalate or Close: How Fast-Tier Models Handle Held-Out Web Attack Families](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08229v1)** both probe lightweight, non-generative "decision models" as cheap defenses against prompt injection and web attacks — a pragmatic direction for scalable guardrails.
- **[A Gingerbread House for LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07611v1)** and **[ScamWorld](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04879v2)** both explore deception-based defenses and fraud-resilience testing for autonomous web agents, reflecting growing concern about agent-vs-agent adversarial dynamics on the open web.
- **[Grounded but Not Immune: Confirmation Bias in Agent-as-a-Judge](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05662v1)** shows that even evaluators with tool access and full trajectory visibility can be swayed by a single unsubstantiated "authority" claim, flipping up to 40% of verdicts — a caution for automated evaluation pipelines increasingly used in safety testing.
- **[Scaling Laws for SAE Training Data](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07756v1)** offers practical guidance that sparse-autoencoder quality plateaus well before training-budget-matched unique activations are needed, lowering the data-engineering bar for interpretability research at scale.
